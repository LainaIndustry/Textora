How to Use a Word Counter for Content Editing

By the Textora Editorial Team Approximately 950 words About 5 minutes to read

A word counter is more useful as part of an editing process than as a number to hit. This guide describes how to use counts alongside other diagnostics.

Counts Are a Diagnostic, Not a Target

The easiest way to misuse a word counter is to treat a target number as a goal. Editing to hit a number often produces padding or unnecessary cuts. The more useful approach is to use counts as signals: they tell you when a draft is unusually short, unusually long or structurally off.

Use the Word Counter for the raw number. Use sentence and paragraph counts alongside it for the fuller picture.

When to Check Word Count

Check at four points in the process:

  1. Planning: set an approximate target per section in the outline.
  2. After the first draft: compare total count to target. Don't edit yet; just note the gap.
  3. After major revisions: confirm the count is still in range.
  4. Before final review: final check against the brief or publication guidelines.

Checking constantly during drafting is distracting. Draft first, count after.

Reading the Combination of Counts

Word count alone is not very informative. Combine it with:

  • Sentence count: average words per sentence = word count ÷ sentence count. Very high averages suggest overly long sentences.
  • Paragraph count: average words per paragraph = word count ÷ paragraph count. Very high averages suggest dense paragraphs.
  • Reading time: helps you judge commitment expected of readers.
  • Unique words: signals vocabulary variety. Low unique counts in long drafts may indicate repetitive phrasing.

The Reading Time Calculator and Keyword Density Checker provide additional context.

When the Draft Is Too Long

If the draft exceeds the target by more than 10%:

  1. Identify sections that don't serve the core argument.
  2. Cut weak examples before cutting strong ones.
  3. Remove redundancy—points stated twice in different words.
  4. Tighten sentences: remove filler words, nominalizations and hedging.
  5. Check word count after each round.

Don't target a precise number; target a range. A draft slightly under the target usually feels better than one that barely fits.

When the Draft Is Too Short

If the draft is below the target by more than 10%:

  1. Check whether the argument is fully developed. A short draft often indicates a missing explanation.
  2. Add concrete examples that strengthen the main points.
  3. Address a counterargument or an edge case.
  4. Add a definition or background paragraph if the audience needs it.
  5. Recount. Stop when the argument is complete.

If the draft is complete but short, consider whether the target is realistic. Sometimes a complete answer is shorter than expected; padding it makes it worse.

Word Count and Genre

Different genres have different expectations for density:

  • News: short sentences, high information density, 300–800 words typical.
  • Blogs: 800–1,500 words for standard posts; longer for guides.
  • Academic essays: 1,000–5,000 words depending on assignment.
  • Reports: 2,000+ words with structured sections.
  • Books: 60,000–120,000 words by genre.

Use these as starting points, not rules. Match your genre and audience.

Character Counts for Specific Formats

Some editing decisions depend on character count:

  • Meta descriptions and titles for SEO.
  • Social media posts.
  • Email subject lines.
  • Push notifications.

The Character Counter gives you the numbers. Pair it with word count when the format has both limits.

Keyword Density as an Editing Signal

If the Keyword Density Checker shows a term appearing at unusually high density, review the relevant passages. Sometimes the repetition is intentional and useful; sometimes it's accidental. Use the tool as a trigger for review, not as a rule to obey.

When to Stop Counting

Stop when the draft meets its targets and reads well. Continuing to check numbers after that point tends to produce unnecessary editing. Trust the draft. Move on to proofreading and publishing.

Conclusion

A word counter is a tool for editing, not a standard for quality. Use it alongside sentence and paragraph counts, reading time and keyword density to see the shape of your draft. The Word Counter and related tools give you the numbers. The judgment about what to do with them is yours.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I edit while drafting to hit a word count?

No. Draft first, count after. Constant counting breaks flow.

Is it better to be under or over a word limit?

Staying within the stated limit is safest. If a range is allowed, aim for the middle.

How do I know if a draft is too long?

Compare to the target and to comparable content in the same genre. Reading time and paragraph length also help.

Does character count matter for a blog post?

Not for the post body, but it does for the title, meta description and social snippets.
