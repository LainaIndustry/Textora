How to Proofread and Edit an Article Before Publishing

By the Textora Editorial Team Approximately 1,000 words About 5 minutes to read

Proofreading is not just a spell check. This checklist covers content, structure, clarity and mechanical errors—the full set of checks before publication.

Editing vs Proofreading

Editing focuses on content, structure and clarity. Proofreading focuses on mechanical errors: typos, punctuation, grammar and formatting. Do them in that order. Editing before proofreading prevents wasted proofreading effort on text that may be cut.

If you are the writer, leave time between finishing the draft and editing it. Even a few hours of distance improves your ability to catch issues.

Stage 1: Content and Structure

Read the article as a whole. Ask:

  • Does the article answer the question it promises?
  • Is the structure logical? Does each section build on the previous one?
  • Does the introduction set expectations that the body fulfills?
  • Does the conclusion do more than repeat the introduction?
  • Are there sections that could be cut without losing meaning?

Fix content and structure first. Word-level polishing is wasted on sections you will remove.

Stage 2: Paragraph and Sentence Level

Move to paragraphs and sentences:

  • Does each paragraph have one clear idea?
  • Is the topic sentence first?
  • Are there transitions between paragraphs?
  • Are there any sentences over 30 words that could be split?
  • Are there nominalizations (“make a decision”) that could be verbed (“decide”)?

Use the Paragraph Counter and Sentence Counter to spot unusually dense sections.

Stage 3: Word Choice

  • Replace vague modifiers (“very,” “really”) with precise language.
  • Check jargon: is each term defined on first use?
  • Remove redundancy (“advance planning,” “past history”).
  • Ensure consistent terminology; don't use two words for the same thing.
  • Watch for accidental repetition. The Keyword Density Checker helps.

Stage 4: Mechanical Errors

  • Spell check, then read manually for homophones (their/there, its/it's).
  • Check subject-verb agreement.
  • Check punctuation, especially commas, semicolons and quotation marks.
  • Verify heading capitalization consistency.
  • Check numbers: consistent style (numerals vs words), correct values, units.
  • Confirm that every link points to a real page.

Stage 5: Factual Accuracy

  • Verify every statistic, quote and claim against a source.
  • Check names, titles and dates.
  • Confirm that any quotations are word-for-word accurate.
  • Ensure links point to the intended destinations, not redirects.

Fabricated statistics and quotations damage trust and can cause legal problems. Verify everything.

Stage 6: Formatting and Consistency

  • Heading levels are correct (H1, H2, H3).
  • Lists, tables and callouts are formatted consistently.
  • Bold and italic are used consistently for the same purposes.
  • Images have descriptive alt text.
  • Metadata (title, description, canonical) is correct for the platform.

Stage 7: Read Aloud

Read the whole article aloud. Reading aloud catches:

  • Awkward rhythm.
  • Repeated phrases you didn't notice silently.
  • Sentences that run out of breath.
  • Missing punctuation.
  • Unclear transitions.

This is often the highest-value editing pass. It is also tiring, so plan for it when you have fresh energy.

Stage 8: Final Checks

  • Read the title and meta description. Do they accurately reflect the article?
  • Check the URL slug for clarity and no typos.
  • Confirm the article works on mobile.
  • Run a link checker.
  • Check word count against any target. Use the Word Counter.

Self-Editing Tips

  • Change the medium: print the article or read on a phone. Different layouts reveal different issues.
  • Read backward: sentence by sentence, from last to first. This disrupts the flow your brain fills in automatically.
  • Set a time limit: avoid endless editing. A stopping point protects quality.
  • Use a checklist: a fixed list catches what memory misses.
  • Have a second reader: someone unfamiliar with the topic catches what you cannot.

Common Errors

  • Skipping editing and going straight to proofreading. Mechanical fixes on an unpolished draft waste effort.
  • Relying on spell check. Spell check misses homophones and context errors.
  • Not reading aloud. Silent reading smooths over problems.
  • Editing endlessly. Past a point, more edits do not improve the article.
  • Ignoring structure. A well-proofread article with poor structure still fails its reader.

Conclusion

Editing and proofreading work best as separate, sequenced stages. Fix content before polishing sentences. Fix sentences before checking mechanics. Read aloud. Verify facts. The Word Counter, Sentence Counter, Paragraph Counter and Keyword Density Checker support the process. The judgment about what to change remains yours.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I wait before proofreading?

Even a few hours helps. A full day is better if the schedule allows.

Should I edit and proofread at the same time?

No. Edit content and structure first, then proofread mechanics. Doing both at once divides attention.

How many editing passes are enough?

Three or four structured passes usually cover the main categories. Additional passes after that rarely improve quality significantly.

What is the most effective single technique?

Reading aloud. It catches rhythm, repetition and missing punctuation that silent reading misses.
