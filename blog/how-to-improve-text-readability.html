How to Improve Text Readability

By the Textora Editorial Team Approximately 1,000 words About 5 minutes to read

Readable text respects the reader's time and attention. This guide covers the practical levers you can pull to make writing easier to absorb.

What Readability Means

Readability combines several things: sentence length, word complexity, paragraph structure, formatting and design. A readable text is easy to follow the first time through, without requiring a re-read. It isn't just about short words; it's about clear structure and consistent rhythm.

Sentence Length

Long sentences are harder to follow. They pile up clauses and force the reader to hold multiple ideas in mind at once. A common guideline is to keep average sentence length under 25 words, with occasional variation.

Compare:

  • Hard: "The report, which was released last week by the committee that oversees regional development, indicates that while several areas have seen improvements, others continue to face challenges related to infrastructure and access to services." (37 words)
  • Easier: "The committee released its report last week. It found improvements in several areas. Other regions still face infrastructure and service access challenges." (24 words, three sentences)

Splitting long sentences is usually the fastest way to improve readability. The Sentence Counter and the Word Counter together give you an average sentence length.

Paragraph Structure

Paragraphs should develop one idea each. In online content, paragraphs of 2–4 sentences work well. Dense paragraphs longer than 150 words are hard to skim. Use the Paragraph Counter to see how many paragraphs a draft contains, then review any paragraph that runs long.

Paragraph breaks also serve a visual purpose. White space between paragraphs gives the reader's eye a place to rest.

Word Choice

Simple words are usually clearer than complex ones, but clarity is context-dependent. In technical writing, precise terms may be necessary. The goal is not to dumb down; it is to reduce friction.

Common friction points:

  • Jargon without definition.
  • Nominalizations: “make a decision” → “decide.”
  • Redundancy: “advance planning,” “past history.”
  • Vague modifiers: “very,” “really,” “quite.”
  • Hedging: “it might possibly be the case that.”

Voice and Active Constructions

Active voice is usually clearer: “The team finished the report” instead of “The report was finished by the team.” Passive voice has its place—when the actor is unknown or unimportant—but excessive passive voice makes text feel distant and bureaucratic.

Formatting and Structure

  • Headings: use descriptive headings to help readers skim.
  • Lists: for sequences or grouped items, a list beats a paragraph.
  • Bold and italic: use sparingly for emphasis; too much defeats the purpose.
  • White space: generous spacing improves perceived readability.
  • Short lines: on the web, aim for 50–75 characters per line.

Readability Metrics

Several formulas estimate readability, including the Flesch-Kincaid grade level and the Flesch Reading Ease score. These use average sentence length and syllable count per word. They are useful as a rough diagnostic, not a target. A piece of content can score well on readability and still fail to communicate if its structure is poor or its ideas are unclear.

Textora does not calculate readability formulas directly, but it gives you the inputs: word count, sentence count and average word length. You can use these with an online formula if you want a numeric score.

Readability for Different Audiences

Different audiences tolerate different levels of complexity. General-audience writing should usually be simpler than content for specialists. A readability pass should consider the reader's expected background:

  • General audience: short sentences, common words, concrete examples.
  • Professional audience: moderate complexity, industry terms are acceptable.
  • Specialist audience: technical vocabulary is expected, but structure still matters.
  • Second-language readers: clear transitions and simple sentences help significantly.

Accessibility Considerations

Readability is an accessibility issue. People with dyslexia, ADHD or low vision may struggle more with dense text. Consider:

  • Avoiding justified text with uneven spacing.
  • Using sufficient contrast between text and background.
  • Avoiding long runs of all caps.
  • Adding clear headings so readers can navigate sections.

Revision Workflow

  1. Finish the draft.
  2. Read it aloud. Note where you stumble or lose track—those are the friction points.
  3. Split any sentence over 30 words.
  4. Break any paragraph over 150 words.
  5. Replace nominalizations and vague modifiers.
  6. Add descriptive headings.
  7. Check sentence and paragraph counts with the tools.
  8. Read again.

Conclusion

Readability is a combination of sentence rhythm, paragraph structure, word choice and formatting. Use the Word Counter, Sentence Counter and Paragraph Counter together to get a quantitative view, then read aloud to catch what the numbers miss.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good average sentence length?

For general audiences, under 20–25 words is often recommended, with occasional variation. The average matters more than any single sentence.

Should I use shorter words?

Usually yes, when a shorter word is equally precise. In technical writing, the precise term may be necessary.

Does readability affect SEO?

Readable content tends to engage readers longer, which can indirectly help. Search engines do not use a single readability score to rank pages.

Are readability formulas reliable?

They are rough estimates. Use them as a diagnostic, not a target. Reading aloud is often more useful than a numeric score.
