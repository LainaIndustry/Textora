How to Check Keyword Density

By the Textora Editorial Team Approximately 1,000 words About 5 minutes to read

Keyword density is one of the oldest SEO metrics, and one of the most misunderstood. This guide explains what it measures, how to calculate it, and why it should inform your editing rather than dictate it.

What Is Keyword Density?

Keyword density is the number of times a given word appears divided by the total number of words in the text, expressed as a percentage:

Density = (Occurrences of the term ÷ Total words) × 100

If “content marketing” appears 8 times in a 1,000-word article, its density is 0.8%. Note that this calculation treats “content marketing” as one term, but our tool analyzes individual words, not phrases. The same formula applies to a single word such as “marketing.”

What the Number Actually Tells You

Density is a descriptive statistic. It tells you how prominent a term is relative to the rest of the text. It does not predict rankings, traffic or conversions. Search engines use many signals—topical relevance, backlinks, user experience, content quality—and no specific density value guarantees a result.

What density is useful for is spotting problems: accidental repetition, keyword stuffing, or a term that dominates a passage unnaturally. It is an editing signal, not a ranking formula.

How to Check Density

  1. Paste the text into the Keyword Density Checker. The Textora Keyword Density Checker shows the most frequent words with counts and percentages.
  2. Note the denominator. Density is calculated against total words after punctuation is stripped. If you exclude stop words, they are removed from the calculation.
  3. Look for outliers. A single word above 4–5% often signals unnatural repetition.
  4. Check for long-tail terms. The tool counts single words, not phrases. For phrase-level analysis, review the text manually.
  5. Read the text aloud. If a term sounds repetitive, it probably reads that way too.

Why Density Varies Between Tools

Different tools use different rules:

  • Denominator: total words with or without stop words.
  • Tokenization: how punctuation and hyphens are handled.
  • Stemming: whether “running” and “runs” are grouped.
  • Phrase handling: some tools count multi-word phrases, others only single words.

Textora uses a simple rule: lowercase the text, strip most punctuation, split on whitespace and count each word. That makes density directly comparable to your word count.

What Is a "Good" Density?

There is no universally correct density. Common range discussions suggest 1–2% for a primary term, but this is a rough heuristic, not a rule. Longer content tends to show lower density for the same term. Short content may show higher density without sounding repetitive.

Rather than targeting a number, ask:

  • Does the term appear where it naturally belongs?
  • Are there synonyms and related concepts, or is the same word repeated mechanically?
  • Does the text read smoothly when spoken aloud?

If yes to all three, density is probably fine regardless of the percentage.

Keyword Stuffing vs Natural Repetition

Natural repetition comes from topic focus: a page about “email marketing” will mention “email” and “marketing” many times because those are the subject. Keyword stuffing is different: it inserts terms where they don't fit, often at the cost of readability. If the text sounds awkward or unnatural, the density is the least of your problems.

Related Concepts

Term Frequency

How often a word appears, without dividing by total words. Our tool shows this alongside density.

TF-IDF

A more advanced statistic that compares a term's frequency in a document to its frequency across a corpus. Our tool does not implement TF-IDF; density alone is a simpler, descriptive signal.

Semantic Relevance

Modern search engines evaluate the meaning of content, not just term counts. Covering a topic well with related terms is more useful than repeating one keyword.

Practical Workflow

  1. Write the draft naturally, without counting.
  2. Read it aloud to catch obvious repetition.
  3. Run a density check for the top 10 words.
  4. Replace mechanical repetition with synonyms or related terms where it improves clarity.
  5. Check the result again if you made significant changes.

Common Mistakes

  • Chasing a target percentage. Density is descriptive, not prescriptive.
  • Ignoring context. A term can be dense because the article is about it.
  • Over-correcting. Removing natural repetition to hit a number can harm clarity.
  • Ignoring readability. A text optimized for density but awkward to read fails its purpose.

Conclusion

Keyword density is a small, useful signal in an editing toolkit. Use the Keyword Density Checker to spot repetition and unnatural term dominance. Then edit for clarity, not for a number. The best density is the one that lets readers absorb the content without noticing it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a safe keyword density?

There is no universal safe number. Aim for natural, readable repetition and review your text aloud.

Does density affect SEO rankings?

Density alone does not determine rankings. Search engines use many signals, and over-optimizing can harm readability.

Does the checker count phrases?

No. It analyzes single words. Phrase-level analysis requires manual review or a specialized tool.

Why does my density change when I exclude stop words?

Excluding stop words removes them from the denominator, which increases the percentage of remaining words.
